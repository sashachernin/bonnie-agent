## The idea

Phoenix residents with an old couch, mirror, refrigerator, or pile of branches need to know what the city will take and what to do when it will not. Build a free, item-first guide that asks what they have, whether it still works, and whether they can transport it, then gives source-dated city, donation, drop-off, and paid-hauling routes. Local haulers pay a proposed flat monthly sponsorship for clearly labelled placement only after the free routes.

## A customer example

Hypothetically, Maya searches “can Phoenix bulk trash take a mirror.” She selects “large unbroken mirror” and learns that ordinary bulk collection excludes glass, sees the relevant city rule, and gets three next actions: offer it for reuse, take permitted material to a transfer station, or request a paid pickup. She chooses a $149-from local hauler because she has no truck. Maya pays the hauler; a future sponsored listing could pay this business.

## Who pays, and for what

The initial user is a Phoenix householder disposing of one bulky item; renters must first confirm that they receive city service. The same first version serves movers, landlords, and relatives clearing a home, but excludes commercial waste and hazardous-material judgment.

Phoenix says its trash system serves more than 400,000 households. The need recurs during moves, replacements, storms, and clean-outs. Residents have asked whether furniture, mirrors, and appointment timing qualify, while the city routes glass, refrigerant appliances, tires, and ordinary bulk differently. The user avoids a rejected pile or unnecessary $95–$149 haul; a hauler pays for an attributable, ready-to-book lead. Search volume and sponsor willingness remain unverified.

## What the AI agent would build

Version one is a fast static site with 40 item pages, a three-question chooser, comparison cards, and dated source links. A small content store records item condition, city eligibility, preparation, resident-only requirements, fees, and alternative routes. Outbound links carry source and sponsor tracking; no address, account, payment, or automated booking is needed.

The coding agent builds the rules engine, structured search markup, accessibility checks, broken-link alerts, and regression tests so “mirror” cannot inherit “furniture.” The hardest risk is policy drift, not code. Version one excludes price quotes, disposal guarantees, hazardous-waste advice beyond official links, and other cities.

## Launch and ongoing maintenance

The owner arranges hosting, analytics, disclosures, and written sponsorship terms. Initial research and entry should take 35–50 hours; allow 8–12 owner hours monthly to check city pages, review corrections, refresh 10 item pages, and contact haulers. The agent can diff sources and flag changes, but the owner approves every rule.

For the first ten users, publish useful pages for the observed queries “can Phoenix bulk trash take a mirror” and “how to get rid of refrigerator Phoenix.” Results currently lead with broad city pages, Reddit uncertainty, and commercial guides; ranking is not verified. The fallback is to ask ten Phoenix moving, decluttering, and neighbourhood newsletter owners for permission to include the free decision link. No placement rules or acceptance were verified, so outreach is a proposed test, not an established channel.

## Why now

The need is established rather than newly created. Phoenix changed to appointment-based bulk pickup in 2024, and its [current rules](https://www.phoenix.gov/administration/departments/publicworks/residential-trash-recycling/bulk-trash-pickup.html) now allow four yearly collections but exclude glass, tires, refrigerant appliances, and several building materials. Current discussions still show appointment and eligibility confusion. The present entry route is item-specific search pages that explain all legal options, not another generic trash-day calendar.

## What exists today

Phoenix bulk pickup is free to eligible city customers, and its transfer stations allow one load up to one ton monthly at no charge; excess disposal is $55 per ton with a $40 minimum. The city is authoritative and should always win on rules, but information is split across service pages. Appliance pickup is $30 for up to five non-refrigerant appliances or $25 per refrigerant item.

[TrashWeek](https://trashweek.com/) is free and covers 79 cities and 4,280 collection zones, while [CurbCalendar](https://curbcalendar.com/) is also free and combines address-based schedules from official sources. Both are better for finding collection day. [AZ Junk Removal](https://azjunkremoval.com/) starts at $149 and reaches $649 for a full truck, providing the paid action after free routes fail. This business can earn attention by answering “where can this exact object go?” rather than “when is trash day?”

## How it makes money

Proposed pricing is $250 per month for one clearly labelled hauler placement in each of four item groups. Four sponsors equal $1,000 monthly revenue. Hosting, monitoring, email, and analytics are estimated at $30–$80 monthly; revenue is not profit. LoadUp confirms that publishers can join its referral programme free through Awin, but publishes no commission, so affiliate income is excluded from the arithmetic.

Assume a sponsor needs a booked job to cost no more than $50. At an assumed 10% click-to-book rate, each needs 50 qualified clicks monthly; four need 200. At an optimistic 20% visitor-to-hauler-click rate that requires 1,000 qualified visits; at a conservative 5%, 4,000. These are sensitivity assumptions, not traffic evidence. Budget 12–20 owner hours monthly for updates, four sponsor reports, support, and ten sales contacts. More item pages and then a second city could continue acquisition without bespoke user work, but editorial effort grows city by city.

## The riskiest assumption

The killing belief is that local haulers will pay $250 monthly for item-specific traffic before the site has substantial volume. In one week, make a source-checked five-item prototype with no sponsor links, recruit 30 Phoenix residents actively disposing of an item within 30 days through permission-based neighbourhood placements, and record their choices. Qualified means city-served or able to state their alternative service. Pass the user test if 20 finish and 10 request a paid quote after seeing free options; fail if 20 finish and fewer than three do. Fewer than 20 is inconclusive. Then show anonymized behavior to ten haulers and ask for a written $250 one-month commitment contingent on 50 qualified clicks; one commitment passes the payer test, none fails it. Do not take payment during this run.

## The part I would argue against

A sceptic would say this is a thin search layer over excellent free city pages, entering results already crowded with schedule sites and hauler-authored guides. They would add that the people most likely to click a hauler are valuable, but no evidence says four businesses will each pay $250 to appear on an unproven site. That objection is decisive unless real users choose paid routes and one hauler commits. A test remains worthwhile because official rules genuinely branch by item, residents still ask item-level questions, and local jobs start around $95–$149; abandon after 20 qualified completions with fewer than three quote requests or ten payer conversations with no contingent commitment.
